Is it fair?

About 5 minutes

An AI learns by looking at lots of examples. These examples are called training data.

If the examples are lopsided, the AI’s choices will be lopsided too. When an AI treats one group worse than another, we call that bias.

Picking the esports team

Hillside Academy wants an AI to pick players for its esports team. Students live in two areas, Town and Village. Both areas have equally good players.

The AI learns from the last 20 players the school picked. Trials used to run after school, when the Village bus had already gone. So most past players came from Town.

The real problem

The school only has 2 past Village players. It can’t find 8 more. What’s the best thing to do?

Is it honest?

About 4 minutes

A chatbot writes by guessing which words are likely to come next. It does not check if they are true.

When an AI makes something up, it’s called a hallucination. The scary part is that it sounds just as sure as when it’s right.

Is it safe?

About 5 minutes

Companies give chatbots rules to stop them doing harm. These rules are called guardrails.

People try to trick chatbots into breaking their rules. That’s called a jailbreak.

Is it private?

About 5 minutes

Anything you type, upload or say to an AI is data. Data about you, like your name, face or address, is called personal data.

Once you press send, it leaves your device. So where does it go?

Would you type it?

Before you type, ask: would I be OK if a stranger read this? Sort each one.

Who protects your data?

The law

UK GDPR, the UK’s data protection law
  • Companies must keep personal data safe and say how they use it. This is in their privacy policy.
  • You can ask a company what data it holds about you.
  • Other people’s personal data is protected too. It’s not yours to upload.

Your habits

Things you control
  • Leave out names, addresses, passwords and photos of people.
  • Check the settings for chat history and whether your chats train the AI.
  • Use the AI tool your school approves, signed in with your school account.

Is it your work?

About 6 minutes

The test: could you explain every sentence to your teacher, without the screen?

Fine, check the rules, or not OK?

Students used AI for school work. Sort each one.

The real rules

There are two sets of rules. They’re not the same, so know which one applies.

Exam board rules

Set by the JCQ, for GCSE and A level coursework
  • The work you hand in must be your own.
  • Using AI so the work isn’t yours is malpractice. That’s the official word for cheating in a qualification.
  • It can mean losing marks, or being disqualified from the qualification.
  • If AI use is allowed, you must say so. Name the tool, and keep a screenshot of what you asked and what it said.
  • Writing just “ChatGPT” as a reference isn’t enough.
  • Any sources the AI gives you, you must check yourself.

Your school’s rules

Set by each school, for homework and class work
  • Every school decides its own rules for homework.
  • Some subjects allow AI. Some don’t allow it at all.
  • If you’re not sure, ask your teacher before you use it, not after.

Even if nobody finds out, there’s no AI in the exam hall. The skills you skipped won’t be there when you need them.

Say how you used it

When AI is allowed, you write a short AI use statement. It tells your teacher exactly what the AI did and what you did. Build one.

Should the school use it?

Which question matters most here?

Be ready to argue

Your school marks about 1,000 pieces of homework a term. If the AI is wrong 5 times in 100, that’s about 50 wrong calls. Who are those 50 students?
Research has found some AI checkers wrongly flag work by students writing in their second language. Is that fair?
Every piece of homework gets sent to the AI company. Where is it stored, and who said yes to that?
If the checker flagged your work by mistake, how could you prove it’s yours?
